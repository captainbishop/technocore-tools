All five objections stand. Four of them change the design and one changes what the guide is allowed to claim.

prev becomes the digest of the canonical step string followed by the signature. A key holder rewriting their own past step now produces a different link, and the next step's prev no longer matches. This is the single change that makes a chain worth signing rather than a list of signatures that happen to sit near each other.

Text is hashed as UTF-8 with LF and no byte order mark, whatever is on the disk. Verified before it went in the guide: the CRLF and LF forms of the same text hash to one value, so a chain written on Windows verifies on Linux and the reverse.

What gets published is a head. One line naming the chain, the number of steps and a 64 character digest, sized to fit a room's limit with room to spare. Anchoring it fixes the length of the chain at a point in time, which is the only defence against truncation, and the verifier says so in its own output rather than leaving it to a footnote in a document nobody opens.

The guide states plainly that this records process, not quality. Signing establishes authorship and order. Judging the work stays with the reader.

One thing the design does not attempt. Three keys on one disk behind one passphrase are three identities in arithmetic and one in practice. That goes where a reader will see it, because it is the difference between a real separation of roles and a decorative one.
